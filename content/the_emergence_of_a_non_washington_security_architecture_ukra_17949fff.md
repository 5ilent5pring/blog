The United States is losing its role as the central coordinator for regional security in the Middle East as Gulf states increasingly turn to Ukraine for counter-drone expertise and defense agreements. This shift follows the deployment of Ukrainian electronic warfare specialists to the Gulf to counter Iranian-designed Shahed drones that have been refined by Russian battlefield testing. The formation of these bilateral security pacts between Ukraine and states like Saudi Arabia and the UAE creates a decentralized defense network that operates independently of traditional U.S. bureaucratic and treaty-based frameworks.

## Argument Mapping

- The author argues that the United States is being bypassed by a new, decentralized security architecture formed by U.S. partners and Ukraine.
  - The central thesis rests on the observation that Gulf states are establishing direct security agreements with Ukraine to mitigate threats that U.S.-led frameworks failed to address. The author supports this with the claim that U.S. bureaucratic friction and unpredictable policy under the Trump administration have forced partners to seek alternative, more agile nodes for defense cooperation. A primary piece of evidence provided is the signing of 10-year security agreements between Ukraine and the UAE, Saudi Arabia, and Qatar.
  - *likely*
- The author's argument depends on the unstated premise that Ukraine possesses a unique, transferable capability in drone and electronic warfare that U.S.-led frameworks cannot replicate.
  - The argument assumes that the specific technical lessons learned by Ukraine against Russian-refined Iranian drones are directly applicable to the Gulf theater. If it were proven that these capabilities are highly context-specific to the Ukrainian frontline and cannot be exported to the Middle East, the necessity for the 'bypass' architecture would vanish. This assessment would change if technical audits showed Ukrainian EW successes are non-transferable to the Gulf's electromagnetic environment.
  - *even odds*

## Devil's Advocacy

- The 'bypass' architecture may actually be a temporary, opportunistic reaction to specific technical gaps rather than a permanent structural shift away from Washington.
  - The author interprets the Ukraine-Gulf agreements as a fundamental change in the international order, but these could be viewed as ad-hoc, single-issue procurement and training efforts. The presence of the 10-year timeframe suggests permanence, but if these agreements remain limited to uncrewed tech and do not expand into broader intelligence or maritime security, the U.S. remains the indispensable hub for all other critical domains. This assessment would be reversed if these partnerships began to include high-end platforms like F-35s or Aegis-level integrated air and missile defense.
  - *likely*
- The author's weakest link is the claim that U.S. partners are intentionally bypassing Washington due to policy unpredictability, ignoring the possibility of domestic Gulf-centric drivers.
  - The author attributes the shift to U.S. 'unpredictability' and 'bureaucratic friction,' yet fails to account for the possibility that Gulf states are pursuing these deals to diversify their own strategic autonomy regardless of U.S. behavior. If evidence emerged that Gulf states were pursuing these deals to reduce dependency on *any* superpower, the author's focus on U.S. policy failures would be secondary to a broader trend of regional hedging.
  - *even odds*

## Evidence Quality Assessment

- The article's argument is persuasive but under-supported due to a reliance on specific anecdotes to represent broad geopolitical shifts.
  - The author uses concrete, verifiable events—such as the deployment of Ukrainian specialists and the signing of 10-year agreements with the UAE and Qatar—to anchor the argument. However, the leap from 'drone cooperation' to a 'new international order' lacks broader empirical support regarding other critical security sectors like maritime or nuclear' security. The argument would be significantly more rigorous if it included data on the decline of U.S. defense procurement volumes in the GCC relative to non-U.S. sources.
  - *persuasive but under-supported*

## What to Watch For

Monitor the expansion of Ukraine-Gulf security agreements beyond uncrewed systems into integrated air defense or maritime surveillance over the next 12-24 months to validate the 'new architecture' thesis. A signal that would invalidate this assessment is the emergence of a major, multi-lateral, non-U.S. led defense framework in the Middle East that excludes Ukraine, suggesting the shift is toward regionalism rather than a specific bypass of Washington via Kyiv.

## Discussion

- How might the integration of Russian-refined Iranian drone technology into the Middle East force a reconfiguration of the 'hub-and-spoke' model used by the U.S. in the Indo-Pacific?
- If the 'bypass' architecture succeeds in the Gulf, what precedent does this set for the future of NATO's ability to maintain centralized command and control during high-intensity conflicts?
