The recent Trump-Xi Jinping summit in Beijing failed to establish a formal great-power condominium, leaving the strategic boundaries of the Indo-Pacific undefined. While the administration avoided explicit concessions on Taiwan, the precedent of approving Nvidia H200 chip sales to China suggests a shift toward unilateral economic concessions. This pattern of behavior facilitates a Chinese sphere of influence through technological and infrastructure integration rather than through formal territorial treaties.

## Argument Mapping

- The author argues that a Chinese sphere of influence in Asia is forming through technological and economic default rather than formal diplomatic agreement.
  - The central thesis rests on the premise that modern power dynamics have shifted from territorial conquest to control over critical technology domains. The author supports this by citing the December 202able approval of Nvidia H200 chip sales to China and Trump's characterization of the Taiwan arms deal as a 'negotiating chip.' The argument depends on the unstated premise that economic and technological interdependence creates a functional sphere of influence even in the absence of political alignment.
  - *almost certainly*

## Devil's Advocacy

- The author's thesis fails to account for the possibility that U.S. military engagement in the Middle East and Iran serves as a deliberate deterrent against Chinese expansion.
  - The author interprets the war in Iran as a 'strategic distraction' that facilitates Chinese gains. However, an alternative interpretation is that the administration's global military posture prevents the very 'closed sphere' the author fears by maintaining U.S. kinetic presence. The analysis lacks the perspective of military strategists who view Middle Eastern volatility as a way to force China into a defensive, regionalized posture. The weakest link is the claim that the H200 chip sale is a precursor to a sphere, as it ignores the possibility of targeted, reversible trade policy.
  - *even odds*

## Evidence Quality Assessment

- The article's argument is persuasive but under-supported due to its reliance on historical analogies to predict future technological shifts.
  - The author uses specific, verifiable events like the Nvidia H200 approval and the Trump-Xi summit to anchor the argument. However, the comparison to the Concert of Europe and the Yalta Conference is speculative, as these were formal, negotiated settlements, whereas the author's subject is a 'sphere by default.' The strength of the argument depends on whether technological integration can truly function as a sphere without the formal political mechanisms the author admits are absent.
  - *persuasive but under-supported*

## What to Watch For

Monitor for specific U.S. policy shifts regarding advanced semiconductor export controls to China over the next six months; a reversal of the H200 precedent would invalidate the 'default sphere' thesis. Conversely, any formalization of a bilateral agreement between Trump and Xi regarding Taiwan's status would confirm the transition toward a negotiated great-power condominium.

## Discussion

- How might the rise of 'digital sovereignty' in the European Union serve as a functional counter-model to the 'open sphere' dynamics described in the Indo-Pacific?
- If the United States adopts a 'Western Hemisphere first' strategy, what specific mechanisms would prevent middle powers in Southeast Asia from gravitating toward China's technological infrastructure?
